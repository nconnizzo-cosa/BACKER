Welcome to the SERP Framework

The State Electronic Records Preservation Framework, a high-level, enterprise-wide guide to preserving digital public records. The State Electronic Records Initiative (SERI) is committed to assisting state and territorial archives increase capabilities for managing and preserving digital records. To create a baseline measurement of capability, each state and territorial archives took a self-assessment survey in 2012, developed as part of the initial SERI grant. As part of subsequent initiatives (four total surveys conducted between 2012-2024), states and territories were asked to complete the self-assessment again in order to longitudinally measure the states’ progress in building digital preservation programs and to gauge the effectiveness of the SERI program. In 2022-2024, as part of the Building Archival Capacity for Keeping Electronic Records (BACKER) grant from the Institute of Museum and Library Services (IMLS), the self-assessment survey was updated and distributed to states and territories to gather more data on their progress. In addition to updating the survey instrument, the underlying preservation framework has been updated as well. The State Electronic Records Preservation (SERP) Framework was originally developed as a tool for state and territorial archives programs to self-evaluate and aid them in improving their digital preservation maturity. Each SERP element was paired with elements of the Digital Preservation Capability Maturity Model (DPCMM) upon which the initial self-assessment survey was based. As part of the 2022 update, the DPCMM now has an enhanced level-up tool for increasing maturity, by-and-large filling the role once held by SERP. That left SERP with no defined role. The SERP framework, therefore, has been re-envisioned as a set of high-level elements that comprise the essential aspects of digital preservation and tiers of maturity defined by benchmarks. SERP’s goal is to address short- and long-term strategic planning elements, provide an organizational structure for the many SERI outputs that support archives in their digital preservation work, and serve as a communication and advocacy tool for state and territorial governments. As digital preservation must occur throughout the records lifecycle, the SERP framework is intended to be used both across the entire enterprise of government as well as within a particular agency or program. SERP is intended to be a pragmatic and practicable framework for digital preservation throughout state and territorial governments. Many factors influence the retention, preservation, and availability of public records throughout their lifecycle, and therefore SERP aims to provide a high-level framework for classifying and addressing those factors systematically. SERP uses this guiding principle to inform its organization and its classifications. Digital public records are of immeasurable value to the people, and without proper care and management is at great risk of degradation, loss, or inaccessibility. SERP is also informed heavily by other standards and models used throughout the archival community, including the aforementioned DPCMM, the Open Archival Information System (OAIS) Model, the National Digital Stewardship Alliance’s Levels of Preservation, and others. The primary concern of SERP is digital preservation: Digital preservation combines policies, strategies, and actions to ensure access to reformatted and born digital content regardless of the challenges of media failure and technological change. The goal of digital preservation is the accurate rendering of authenticated content over time. Preservation of the data that comprises the body of public information, with the implicit understanding that preserved data must be accessible to have value, will ensure that policymakers, voters, and future generations of citizens have access to the records that document the decisions of their public officials. This goes beyond technical measures alone – a robust policy framework that necessarily includes multiple agencies collaborating and efficient, repeatable, and well-documented processes will ensure that preservation is effective. Preservation of data and context cannot be performed alone.

Framework Elements Each of the core elements of the State Electronic Records Preservation framework represents a high-level group of related domain areas with unique challenges, but each element is interrelated with factors from the other. Because these elements are necessarily connected, the framework does not recognize high achievement in one area alone as being sufficient preservation. All elements must be addressed by the organization and the enterprise (the collaborative group of public agencies involved in these processes) to achieve high levels of maturity: Identifying Essential Records and Recordkeeping Requirements States must control their records to manage their records. Control requires a complete inventory of records and systems in which they reside. Preservation of essential records necessitates identifying what elements of records are essential. Digital records have form and structure beyond their content, including metadata and relationships to other records, that are essential to their understanding Legal requirements (including retention and disposition), technical requirements (format, applications, and workflows), administrative requirements (approval, custody, and control), and security requirements (protecting PII, PHI, intellectual property) are just some of the essential requirements for effective public records management. Requirements for managing records should be documented in standardized forms and must make these requirements broadly available and accessible to both the administrators and audiences of public records. Assigning Roles and Responsibilities for Managing Digital Records Records are managed by many custodians throughout their life cycles, from those who create and receive records directly, to those who manage systems in which records are stored, and those who author, implement, and audit governance. Those who are closest to the creation and receipt of records are typically those with the most knowledge of their form and content. Failure to meet requirements at any point in the lifecycle can irreparably damage the authenticity, reliability, or integrity of records. Most digital preservation and governance tasks are best assigned to the records’ custodians. Governing Digital Systems and Services Digital records and data are stored within systems and manipulated by applications and services that create, modify, duplicate, reformat, and destroy them. Control and documentation of these systems is essential to adequately preserve records today, and contextualize them tomorrow. How systems are configured matters as much as which systems are in place. Digital systems include more than software or hardware components; systems include the policies, procedures, and standards that define the pathways through which digital information flows. Public agencies rely heavily on external vendors to provide most of the systems that manage public records. Control of the information in these systems is paramount, and ensuring that these systems can comply with digital recordkeeping requirements is an ever-evolving problem. Coordination between various systems-related roles (developers, procurement, vendors, system administrators) and records producers is key to ensuring that all digital systems are properly governed. Migrating Essential Records and Data Government is made of many agencies with many employees, and is subject to near-constant turnover of laws, regulations, and leadership. Migration of data from one custodian, system, format, or structure to another must be a core component of any governance framework. Identifying the significant properties of digital records, the value of data elements, and how to preserve them during migrations is key to the practice of digital preservation. It is possible to preserve too much information and do so inefficiently. Public recordkeeping systems should be designed with interoperability, information transfer, and flexibility as core goals, in addition to information security and discoverability. Vendor-produced systems must allow for the non-destructure export of records to open, preservation-ready formats. Most business systems are not designed to retain data for the extended time frames. Permanent records must be migrated to trustworthy repositories for permanent preservation. Providing Access to Records Records must be available to the designated audience communities to have their value realized. If the public cannot access digital information in a timely fashion, then its value is degraded or effectively destroyed. Archives, as permanent repositories of information, must constantly evaluate how the public seeks, interprets, and contextualizes records within. Public records frequently appear in legal contexts, where accuracy and completeness of chain of custody is paramount. Digital records should be accompanied with the details of their management and provenance in order to fully trust the information contained within.
